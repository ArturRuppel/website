Artur Ruppel

Experimental biophysicist
Institut Pasteur, Paris

I am an engineer by training, and I started my scientific journey in Grenoble, studying the mechanics of force transmission between epithelial cells. There I became fascinated by how cells build structures many times their own size during development, and so in Montpellier I turned to the elementary unit of tissue morphogenesis: the T1 transition, in which cells exchange neighbours. The next step was the scale of the whole tissue. In 2025 I joined Institut Pasteur to address tissue mechanics and organisation in cancer, where the tight homeostatic control of a healthy tissue goes awry.

Throughout, my approach has stayed the same: I build minimal tissues, from two cells to several thousand, in which forces and shapes can be measured directly. Micropatterning sets the geometry, optogenetics lets me switch contractility on and off, traction force microscopy gives me the forces, and segmentation and tracking follow every cell, with simulations alongside to make sense of the measurements. What occupies me now is mechanical heterogeneity. The cells of a tumour differ in stiffness, contractility and adhesion, and I want to understand how these differences between individual cells set the state of the tissue as a whole.

Research

Mechanical heterogeneity in cancer cell collectives

Current work · Institut Pasteur · with Sandrine Etienne-Manneville

No two cells in a tissue are the same. In a tumour the differences are stark: soft cells next to stiff ones, regions that flow next to regions that are stuck. Physics has models for how a collective of cells can flow like a fluid or arrest like a solid, and these models make a prediction I find remarkable: a mixture does not behave like the average of its components, and where the different cells sit can matter more than how many of them there are. I want to know whether this holds in a living tissue, and how the mechanical differences between individual cells set the state of the whole.

To find out, I build tissues in which the heterogeneity can be controlled. I take two populations of glioblastoma cells that differ in a single component of the cytoskeleton, vimentin, and study them alone and mixed, from pairs of cells to tissues of several thousand. At every scale I measure the shapes, movements and forces of the individual cells alongside the behaviour of the tissue as a whole, and I run simulations of deformable cells, in which the kind and the arrangement of heterogeneity can be controlled more directly, to connect the two.

The elementary unit of tissue rearrangement

CRBM, Montpellier · with François Fagotto, Martial Balland and Lisa Manning

A tissue changes shape by letting its cells exchange neighbours. This process, cell intercalation or the T1 transition, is how a tissue can deform a great deal and still hold together. Two mechanisms are known to drive it: the junction between two cells contracts, or cells migrate in between their neighbours. Theory says that every exchange has to cross an energy barrier, and that the height of this barrier decides whether the tissue flows or arrests. The trouble is that in a tissue the exchanges are mechanically coupled to one another, and so the barrier of a single event had never been measured.

We isolated a single event by confining four cells on a cross-shaped micropattern. These quadruplets exchange neighbours on their own, and the forces can be followed throughout: interfacial tensions from the shapes of the cells, migratory forces from traction force microscopy. We found that both tension and migration drive the exchange, in MDCK epithelial cells as well as in Xenopus mesoderm cells. From the distribution of junction lengths we obtained an effective energy landscape with a cusp at the point of exchange, just as vertex models predict. And when we elongated the pattern, the barrier rose in proportion to the strain and exchanges became rarer: geometry controls how readily cells rearrange.

Ruppel, Misiak et al., Nature Physics (2026)

Force propagation between epithelial cells

LIPhy, Grenoble · with Martial Balland, Thomas Boudou and Ulrich Schwarz

Cells in a tissue coordinate what they do through the forces they exert on one another. Mechanical signals travel faster than biochemical ones and can have much the same effect. Yet nobody had followed the chain of events quantitatively: one cell generates a force, the force crosses the contact between the cells, and the neighbour responds. This was the question of my PhD.

We built a minimal system in which the chain can be followed: two MDCK epithelial cells on an H-shaped micropattern. With optogenetics we made one cell contract on demand, and with traction force microscopy we measured how its neighbour responded. The neighbour, it turned out, actively contracts in its own right. A single cell of the same size behaves differently: its actin network flows instead of building up stress. How strongly the neighbour responds depends on how the cell pair is mechanically and structurally polarised, which we could set through the shape of the micropattern. The response is strongest when the polarisation is perpendicular to the direction in which the force travels, and the same holds in small tissues of a few hundred cells. And so active response and cellular organisation allow mechanical signals to keep their strength as they travel from cell to cell.

Ruppel, Wörthmüller et al., eLife (2023)

Software

I build the software I need for my research and make it available on my GitHub when it is ready to be used by others.

napariTFM

Published · open source

A napari plugin for traction force microscopy and monolayer stress microscopy. It covers the complete analysis, from bead displacements to traction forces and internal stresses, for single cells and monolayers, on single images or time series.

A. Ruppel et al., PLOS Computational Biology 22, e1014045 (2026) · Paper · Code · Test data

ITASC

Ready to use · open source

A napari plugin that segments and tracks cells in dense, highly motile monolayers, where automatic methods break down. It combines Cellpose and Ultrack with interactive correction adapted from EpiCure, and gives every cell and its nucleus one identity that holds across the whole recording.
