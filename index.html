Artur Ruppel

Experimental biophysicist
Institut Pasteur, Paris

Living systems are organised in layers, from molecules to cells to tissues, and each layer has properties that its parts do not have on their own. I study the step from cells to tissues: how local interactions between neighbouring cells give rise to the collective behaviour of the tissue.

My approach is to reconstitute minimal tissues, from two cells to several thousand, in which forces and shapes can be measured directly. My current work is on mechanical heterogeneity: how differences in the mechanical properties of individual cells shape the state of the tissue as a whole.

Research

Mechanical heterogeneity in cancer cell collectives

Current work · Institut Pasteur

The cells of a tissue are never identical. They differ in stiffness, contractility and adhesion, and in tumours these differences are pronounced: soft and stiff cells, fluid and solid regions coexist in the same tissue. Physical models explain how a collective of cells can flow like a fluid or arrest like a solid. In these models, a mixture does not simply behave like the average of its components, and the spatial arrangement of the different cells can matter more than their proportions. My question is how this plays out in a living tissue: how the mechanical differences between individual cells set the state of the whole.

I approach this by building tissues in which the heterogeneity is controlled. Two populations of glioblastoma cells that differ in one component of the cytoskeleton, vimentin, are studied alone and mixed, from pairs of cells to tissues of several thousand. At each scale, I measure the shapes, movements and forces of the individual cells together with the behaviour of the tissue as a whole. Simulations of deformable cells, in which the kind and the arrangement of heterogeneity can be varied at will, connect the two.

The elementary unit of tissue rearrangement

CRBM, Montpellier

Tissues change shape by letting cells exchange neighbours. This process, cell intercalation, allows large deformations while the tissue stays coherent. Two mechanisms are known to drive it: contraction of the junction between two cells, and migration of cells in between their neighbours. Theory predicts that each exchange has to cross an energy barrier, and that the height of this barrier decides whether a tissue flows or arrests. In a tissue, however, exchanges are mechanically coupled to one another, and the barrier of a single event had remained out of experimental reach.

We isolated a single event by confining four cells on a cross-shaped micropattern. These quadruplets exchange neighbours spontaneously, and the forces can be measured throughout: interfacial tensions from cell geometry, migratory forces from traction force microscopy. Both tension and migration drive the exchange. This holds for MDCK epithelial cells and for Xenopus mesoderm cells, which intercalate in the embryo. The distribution of junction lengths gives an effective energy landscape with a cusp at the point of exchange, as vertex models predict. Elongating the pattern raises the barrier in proportion to the strain and makes exchanges rarer: geometry alone controls how readily cells rearrange.

Ruppel, Misiak et al., Nature Physics (2026)

Force propagation between epithelial cells

LIPhy, Grenoble

Cells in a tissue coordinate their behaviour through the forces they exert on one another. Mechanical signals travel faster than biochemical ones and can have similar effects. Yet the chain of events had not been described quantitatively: one cell generates a force, the force crosses the contact between the cells, and the neighbouring cell responds.

We built a minimal system in which this chain can be followed: two MDCK epithelial cells on an H-shaped micropattern. Using optogenetics, we made one cell contract on demand and measured the response of its neighbour by traction force microscopy. The neighbour responds with a contraction of its own, which a passive material cannot account for, and the pair deforms as a coherent unit. A single cell of the same size behaves differently: its actin network flows instead of building up stress. The strength of the response depends on how the pair is polarised, mechanically and structurally, which we controlled through the shape of the micropattern: it is strongest when the polarisation is perpendicular to the direction of force propagation. The same holds in small tissues. Active response and cellular organisation thus allow mechanical signals to keep their strength as they travel.

Ruppel, Wörthmüller et al., eLife (2023)

Software

I build the software I need for my research and make it available on my GitHub when it is ready to be used by others.

napariTFM

Published · open source

A napari plugin for traction force microscopy and monolayer stress microscopy. It covers the complete analysis, from bead displacements to traction forces and internal stresses, for single cells and monolayers, on single images or time series.

A. Ruppel et al., PLOS Computational Biology 22, e1014045 (2026) · Paper · Code

ITASC

Ready to use · open source

A napari plugin that segments and tracks cells in dense, highly motile monolayers, where automatic methods break down. It combines Cellpose and Ultrack with interactive correction adapted from EpiCure, and gives every cell and its nucleus one identity that holds across the whole recording.
